# Product Case Frameworks

## Truth-grounded knowledge expansion checklist

Before outputting a product-case拆解, explicitly check:

- What in the selected note is a fact vs the note author's opinion?
- What outside/domain knowledge is needed to make the analysis real?
- What competitor gap or alternative user behavior explains the opportunity?
- What user psychology or behavior shift makes the product choice work?
- What exact product/data/operation mechanism converts the insight into retention, conversion, supply, or growth?
- Which claims are inference and should be labeled as such?
- Which facts are dated, niche, controversial, or likely to require web/source verification?

Output should teach the user something new; do not just repackage the provided text.

## Opportunity / demand analysis checklist

- Target user: specific role/persona, not “all users”.
- Scenario: when/where the pain appears.
- Pain: urgency, cost of not solving, emotional/business consequence.
- Alternatives: current workaround and why it fails.
- MVP: smallest proof of willingness to use/pay/return.
- Core hypothesis: demand, trust, supply, channel, conversion, retention.
- Expansion: from one scenario to adjacent scenarios.

## Two-sided marketplace checklist

### Basic structure
- Minimum transaction unit.
- Supply side and demand side.
- Key side: which side missing makes the other side leave immediately.
- Atomic network: smallest geography/community/category/time window where liquidity can form.

### Liquidity metrics
- Supply count and effective supply rate.
- Search result rate / zero-result rate.
- Contact or booking rate.
- Match success rate.
- Average response time.
- Cancellation / dispute rate.
- Repeat usage and satisfaction.

### Trust mechanisms
- Identity verification.
- Authentic photos/materials.
- Reviews and ratings.
- Payment escrow/guarantee.
- Customer support and dispute process.
- Platform rules, penalties, and risk controls.

### Supply quality dimensions
- Quantity: enough options.
- Quality: attractive, accurate, reliable.
- Availability: actually bookable/usable.
- Response: timely interaction.
- Economics: supply side earns enough to stay.

## Success / failure attribution checklist

- Structural timing: market, technology, policy, behavior, or channel shift.
- User insight: underserved segment, strong scenario, unmet emotional/business job.
- Differentiation: what it did unlike incumbents and why that mattered.
- Validation proof: MVP, retention, conversion, repeat usage, supply growth, or willingness to pay.
- Execution/resource leverage: team capability, distribution, data, algorithm, content, capital, partnerships, or operations.
- Feedback loop: data loop, content loop, supply-demand loop, social/network effect, or brand loop.
- Defensibility: why competitors cannot immediately copy the result, or why copying hurts them.
- Counterfactual: what would have made it fail, and which risk was actually solved.

## Stage-gate signals

- Continue: small group strongly loves it, repeats, recommends, or tolerates rough edges.
- Pivot: pain exists but user segment, scenario, channel, or product form is wrong.
- Scale: one atomic network has stable liquidity and repeatable acquisition/supply playbook.
- Stop: even high-intensity scenarios cannot produce the key behavior, or unit economics never closes.

## Six-question full-case checklist

For full product/company cases, verify these before finalizing:

1. **What did it do?** Product form, target user/customer, key workflow, business model, minimum successful action.
2. **Why did it succeed?** Multi-factor attribution: trend, pain, wedge, mechanism, GTM/supply, feedback loop, team capability, timing.
3. **What did it capture versus competitors?** Direct competitors, indirect substitutes, old workflow, customer-built solution, and the gap it exploited.
4. **How did it evolve after 0-1?** MVP → repeatable playbook → platformization/commercialization/ecosystem/strategic shift.
5. **What problems appeared during growth?** Supply, quality, cost, trust, monetization, regulation, customer concentration, competition, organization.
6. **How did it solve them?** Productization, SOP/tooling, governance, trust mechanisms, data isolation, quality systems, strategic upmarket move.

## Competitor vs opportunity branch

- If competitors/substitutes exist: use **old solution → remaining gap → new wedge → mechanism → why hard to copy → proof**.
- If competitors/substitutes exist: do not include a no-competitor branch. Use **market change → customer task → core bottleneck → old solution gap → product scheme → MVP loop → landing operations → productized mechanism → expansion**.
- If no obvious competitor truly exists: use **trend → founder/market pain → repeated inefficiency → infrastructure gap → why now → first painful users → proof**.

Do not invent competitors just to fill a table. But also do not label a case “no obvious competitor” when substitutes such as manual workflows, internal tools, spreadsheets, agencies, open-source tools, customer self-build, or doing nothing exist.

## Post-0-1 stage analysis

| Stage | What to inspect | Problem pattern | Solution pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-1 | MVP, first users, minimum loop | demand uncertainty, weak channel, insufficient supply | manual wedge, atomic market, tight feedback |
| 1-10 | replication playbook | manual work cannot scale, quality variance | SOP, dashboards, tooling, role specialization |
| 10-100 | platform/commercialization | trust, cost, copycats, governance | rules, QA systems, brand/ecosystem, automation |
| strategic shift | new tech/market | core business commoditization, customer shift | move up value chain, migrate capabilities |
| mature stage | multi-line/org/regulation | customer concentration, neutrality, compliance, org complexity | data isolation, compliance, org layers, risk governance |


## High-value wedge problem checklist

Use when selecting a 0-1 entry market or when the case says “find the most painful/expensive/difficult/willing-to-pay customer problem.” Do not equate “big market” with “good wedge.” A good wedge is small enough to dominate but painful enough to pay.

### Four dimensions

| Dimension | Definition | Strong signals | Weak/false signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Most painful | The problem blocks a core KPI or mission-critical workflow, not just convenience | executive attention, repeated workaround, delivery/revenue/safety/growth blocked | users say they like it but no behavior changes |
| Most expensive | Cost of inaction is visible in money, time, opportunity, risk, compliance, or churn | existing spend, headcount, delayed projects, SLA/safety/legal risk, calculable ROI | large TAM but low per-customer loss |
| Most difficult | Existing solutions fail because the job requires complex tech/ops/data/quality/trust/integration | competitors solve only one point, customer self-build is heavy, quality bar is high | hard but not valuable, or impossible to deliver with MVP |
| Most willing to pay | Budget owner has urgency, authority, and a measurable business case | paid workaround, paid pilot, procurement motion, recurring need, buyer/user alignment | user is painful but not budget owner; budget owner cannot see value |

### Formula

```text
high-value wedge = pain intensity × cost of inaction × willingness to pay × defensible difficulty ÷ adoption friction
```

Use behavior evidence over stated interest:口头喜欢 < 愿意试用 < 提供真实数据 < 使用 workaround < 已为替代方案付费 < 不解决影响收入/安全/合规/交付.

### SOP

1. List concrete customer roles and tasks, not broad industries.
2. Identify which core KPI the problem affects: revenue, cost, speed, risk, compliance, safety, retention, growth.
3. Estimate cost of inaction: labor, delay, opportunity, error, risk, compliance, churn.
4. Check current workaround and existing spend.
5. Identify buyer, user, budget owner, and decision urgency.
6. Diagnose why existing solutions fail: tech, ops, data, quality, trust, integration, regulation.
7. Choose a high-density wedge: fewer customers is fine if pain, budget, repeat usage, and proof value are high.
8. Validate with paid pilots or real workflow/data, not survey interest.


## Why this company can but others cannot

Use when the user asks “为什么它可以，别人不行？” or when a case needs defensibility beyond surface success.

Analyze five layers:

| Layer | Question |
|---|---|
| Task choice | Did it choose a task that is hard enough to create a barrier but valuable enough for customers to pay? |
| System integration | Does it combine multiple point capabilities into an end-to-end workflow? |
| Result responsibility | Is it selling a tool/labor input, or taking responsibility for a business/model outcome? |
| Supply/productization | Can it recruit, train, route, grade, and retain the critical supply side? |
| Workflow embedding | Is it embedded in the customer's recurring core workflow, creating switching cost and feedback loops? |
| Capability migration | Can the capability move to the next demand wave, or is it trapped in one vertical? |

Template:

> Others only had 【point capability】, such as labor, tools, or vertical service. This company won because it combined 【capability A + capability B + capability C】 into 【end-to-end workflow】, took responsibility for 【customer outcome】, embedded into 【core recurring workflow】, and used feedback to improve 【system/flywheel】. The moat is not the surface feature, but the integrated operating system behind it.

Checklist:
- Compare point capability vs system capability.
- Compare input sale vs outcome responsibility.
- Compare one-off project vs recurring workflow embedding.
- Compare generic supply vs routed/graded/managed supply network.
- Compare vertical dependency vs cross-wave capability migration.


## Product transformation judgment framework

Use when analyzing how a product/company changes with the时代, why it transformed, what choices it made, and what results those choices created.

### Transformation signals

| Signal | Question |
|---|---|
| Customer bottleneck migration | Has the customer's next biggest problem moved downstream/upstream? |
| Core business commoditization | Is the original product/service becoming cheaper, easier to copy, or self-built? |
| Technology shift | Does a new technology change the required data, workflow, or user behavior? |
| Capability transferability | Can the company's existing assets/capabilities transfer to the new opportunity? |
| Value-chain movement | Is there a more valuable adjacent layer closer to budget, decision, deployment, or risk ownership? |
| Risk of staying still | What happens if the company keeps optimizing the old business only? |

### Choice-result table

For each transformation stage, fill:

| Stage | Era change | Choice made | Path not chosen | Result | New risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |  |  |

### Transformation SOP

1. Identify the external shift: technology, user behavior, cost, regulation, channel, competition.
2. Identify the customer's new bottleneck after the old problem is partially solved.
3. Diagnose whether the old business is being commoditized.
4. Identify the transferable core capability: data, trust, supply, workflow, brand, algorithm, channel, distribution, compliance.
5. Choose direction: move up value chain, move down to cheaper mass market, or expand horizontally to adjacent scenarios.
6. Evaluate each choice's result and new risk.
7. Validate the new direction with a wedge/MVP and real willingness to pay.
8. Connect the new business back to the existing flywheel; avoid unrelated diversification.

Template:

> Transformation is not chasing a trend. It is recognizing that the customer's bottleneck has moved from 【old bottleneck】 to 【new bottleneck】, while the company's reusable capability is 【capability】. The company chose 【new direction】 instead of 【rejected path】, which created 【result】 but also introduced 【new risk】.


## Market-demand to product-scheme construction path

Use when a case has competitors/substitutes and the user wants to know how the 0-1 product was thought through and built.

```text
market/technology change → customer task → core bottleneck → old solution gap → entry wedge → product scheme → MVP loop → landing operations → productized mechanism → expansion path
```

Checklist:
- What changed externally?
- What task did the target customer need to complete?
- What was the bottleneck blocking the task?
- Why were existing competitors/substitutes insufficient?
- Which entry customer/scenario was chosen and why?
- What product scheme/system design was written, not just what feature was built?
- What MVP loop proved the scheme?
- Which manual operations enabled early landing?
- Which operations were productized into workflow/rules/tools/data flywheel?
- What result or expansion path came from this choice?

## Named competitor differentiation matrix

Use when the user asks for competitor comparison, or when the case has several modern competitors that look similar on the surface. Do not stop at broad categories like “tools / BPO / SaaS.” Add a named matrix when it materially improves understanding.

### When to use

- The case has 3–5 directly comparable companies/products.
- The user needs long-term learning, not only an interview sound bite.
- The surface category is confusing, e.g. all companies seem to be “data labeling,” “AI agent platform,” “content platform,” or “marketplace.”
- The strategic question is: **which control point in the value chain did each company occupy?**

### Comparison dimensions

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| One-sentence positioning | What exact job does this company want to own in the customer's workflow? |
| Customer JTBD | What uncertainty or pain does the customer delegate to this vendor? |
| Value-chain control point | Does it own data, workflow, supply, quality, distribution, evaluation, deployment, or trust? |
| Product form | Tool/SaaS, managed service, marketplace, expert network, infrastructure, API, platform, or vertical solution? |
| Supply / quality mechanism | How does it recruit, route, grade, review, or automate the critical supply/input? |
| Workflow depth | Is it a point tool, a production workflow, or an end-to-end outcome loop? |
| Best-fit customer | Which customer segment has the strongest urgency and willingness to pay? |
| Moat | What is hard to copy: data, experts, workflow embedding, trust, compliance, distribution, brand, or operations? |
| Risk / weakness | What could make customers switch, self-build, or downgrade? |
| PM memory label | A short label that makes the difference memorable. |

### Canonical pattern from Scale AI case

- **Scale AI**: end-to-end data flywheel / reliable AI infrastructure.
- **Surge AI**: expert human intelligence / high-quality RLHF data.
- **Labelbox**: AI data factory platform + expert service network.
- **Encord**: multimodal / physical AI / medical-vision data workflow platform.

The goal is not to memorize these four names, but to learn the method: **compare which uncertainty each company absorbs for the customer** — quality, supply, workflow, model outcome, evaluation, deployment, or trust.

## Real early-platform pain analysis

Use for infrastructure, platform, AI data, B2B workflow, marketplace, developer-tool, and operations-heavy cases. The goal is to uncover what was actually hard to build in 0-1, instead of only saying “it built a platform.”

### Core question

```text
What messy human/manual/business process had to be stabilized before it could become a product or platform?
```

### Pain dimensions

| Pain layer | Question | Typical productization mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Task definition | Did customers even know the exact standard, taxonomy, acceptance criteria, or desired output? | templates, examples, calibration, requirements workshops, schema/ontology |
| Workflow splitting | Can the job be decomposed into small, parallel, auditable units? | task pipeline, subtask routing, versioning, merge/review workflow |
| Supply organization | Who performs the work, and how are they recruited, trained, routed, graded, and retained? | supply tiers, onboarding tests, reputation, task matching, expert pools |
| Quality control | How is correctness measured before the customer sees the result? | golden tasks, consensus, review layers, audits, benchmark sets, QA dashboards |
| Cost / efficiency | How does the product avoid becoming a low-margin service business? | automation, pre-labeling, prioritization, sampling, workflow reuse |
| Outcome proof | How does the vendor prove the work improved the customer's real KPI, not just completed a task? | evaluation loop, model/business metric tracking, before/after tests |
| Trust / risk | Why would a customer hand over sensitive data/workflow to an external platform? | permissions, data isolation, compliance, audit logs, security review |
| Repeatable productization | Which manual project-management actions become repeatable tools, rules, dashboards, or APIs? | SOP, workflow config, reusable templates, monitoring, self-serve controls |

````markdown
## Real early-platform pain: what was actually hard?

### Minimum loop

```text
customer raw input → define standard → small calibration → find ambiguity → revise workflow/QA → production run → review/consensus → delivery → customer outcome feedback → update standard/workflow
```
````

### Canonical pattern from Scale AI case

Scale AI's early labeling-platform pain was not “draw boxes on images.” It was converting subjective human judgment into stable ML input:

```text
customer unclear data need → task/taxonomy definition → worker/expert routing → multi-stage QA → labeled data delivery → model evaluation → failure sample feedback → next-round instruction/QA update
```

This pattern should be generalized to other cases: identify the hidden operational mess, then show how it became a repeatable product system.
